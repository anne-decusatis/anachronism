When I was an aspiring youth rapier combatant, I was unable to regularly participate in SCA combat because I was several hours' drive from the nearest practice, and it wasn't a viable option on a weeknight for a parent to take me 2-3 hours each way to a different state to attend the closest practice to us. Now that I am an adult, I would like to have the opportunity to improve access in whatever ways I can, in order for the next generation of youth combatants to have more options than I did. As such, I have been working on getting signed off as a youth rapier marshal. I am currently a Marshal-in-Training for youth, and it was going slowly until October, when a youth interested in rapier started attending one of our local practices. Now I am putting more time into chatting with the Kingdom Youth Rapier Marshal to get my i's dotted and t's crossed, and trying to figure out what to do and how to do it in order to safely practice with the youth. 

## Safe practices

1. It's important for the youth to be able to identify the adults involved in a practice by name, should something go wrong. Recommendation for a game to ensure everyone knows everyone's names: Start warm-up by tossing a ball from person to person. Say the name of the person when you throw the ball to them. You can call "hold" during this to practice behavior for calling holds too. 

2. We have a "two deep" rule for working with youth, which requires two adults present who aren't related to each other (i.e. not me and my wife), one of whom is background checked, when youth are present. I have now been background checked by the SCA process twice now (2024, and 2026 - it is valid for two years). It was additionally strongly recommended to me to have an adult whom is not related to me or the youth in question *actually looking at us, not just present in the room* every time I have to get close to or physically touch the youth (e.g. armor inspection).

3. Depending on age, youth can miss adjectives/qualifiers in sentences. E.g. "Don't hit harder than the minimum necessary" -> "don't" can get lost in their memory. Try to frame information positively rather than negatively - "do" rather than "don't".

4. I am not allowed to spar with youth outside the supervision of a youth rapier marshal until I am out of training and am signed off as a full youth rapier marshal. Once I am a full marshal, I will be allowed to run events (not just practices) with youth combat, and I will be allowed to both spar and designate others I trust to spar with youth. Note that in the youth combat rules, free sparring is treated differently than "youth hits armored adult to practice calibration of shots", which I am currently allowed to participate in. 

5. Youth should learn what armor they need, but pieces can often get lost during water breaks, adult marshals present need to confirm with youth that they are properly attired every time they are on the field. 

## Fun practices

1. Youth attention spans are lower than adults - plan for 45 minutes to an hour of content at most. 

2. Youth respond well to games and won't be able to focus as strongly as adults on non-game drills.

3. Add on to games they may already know from the rest of their lives - for example, "Simon Says". (Specifically it was recommended to me to adapt this game for footwork practice, which I did have to look up afterward to ensure I knew the rules, as it wasn't something I played by that name as a child: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What%27s_the_time,_Mr_Wolf%3F>)

4. One other recommended bladework practice game is that you can throw scarves and ask them to lunge and use the tip of the sword to pin them to a wall. I will have to find some scarves for this. 

I am scrambling a bit to figure out what content to put in my practices that won't be boring, especially at the smaller practice where the youth is attending when there are really only a few people interested in youth drills (me, the youth, the parent, maybe Aife, and whomever else I need to rope in to be an unrelated adult witness). Tips are welcome!
